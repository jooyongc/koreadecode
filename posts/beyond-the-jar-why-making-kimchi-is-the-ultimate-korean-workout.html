Beyond the Jar: Why Making Kimchi is the Ultimate Korean Workout

Mon Jan 26 2026

When I first moved to Seoul five years ago, I thought I was a true kimchi aficionado. I ordered the spicy cabbage with everything, knew the difference between *kkakdugi* (radish cubes) and *oi sobagi* (cucumber kimchi), and could handle the heat. But there was one huge, spicy hurdle I hadn’t cleared: actually making it.

I always figured kimchi making was reserved for the legendary Korean grandmothers (*halmeonis*) whose hands moved with lightning speed, powered by generations of spice mastery. The process felt intimidating, almost sacred. But trust me, once you live here long enough, you realize that to truly decode Korea, you have to get your hands messy. So, this past November, I finally signed up for a Kimjang workshop. And guys, it was the best cultural and literal workout I’ve had all year.


Photo by huiyeon kim on Unsplash

The Real Scoop: What is Kimjang?

Kimjang (김장) isn’t just a recipe; it’s a huge annual event. Historically, before refrigerators were common, families and communities would gather in late autumn to make enough kimchi to last the entire harsh Korean winter. It’s such an essential piece of Korean cultural identity that it’s actually recognized by UNESCO. It’s about survival, community, and sharing the labor (and the delicious results).

I joined a workshop where we tackled hundreds of heads of salted Napa cabbage. The first step, which is often done ahead of time, is painstakingly salting the cabbage and letting it sweat out moisture for hours—this is crucial for that perfect crunch! But the main event is the *yangnyeom* (the paste).

Picture this: huge tubs filled with red pepper flakes (*gochugaru*), minced garlic (and I mean buckets of garlic), ginger, fermented fish sauce (*aekjeot*), sometimes salted shrimp, and finely shredded radishes and scallions. The smell alone is electrifying. It’s intense, pungent, and instantly recognizable as the true smell of Korean winter prepping.

Why I Love It (The Best Cultural Vibe)

My favorite part wasn’t learning the exact ratio of anchovy sauce to garlic (though that was fascinating). It was the energy. Everyone, young and old, was elbow-deep in the bright red paste. We were wearing giant rubber gloves and laughing because someone inevitably splattered chili paste onto their forehead. It’s communal labor at its finest.

The physical effort is no joke, either. You have to meticulously smear that paste onto every single leaf of every single cabbage head. By the end, my shoulders were tired, and my hands were stained red, even through the gloves. It’s truly a marathon, not a sprint.

But the ultimate payoff? Tasting the freshly made kimchi, known as *geotjeori* (겉절이), before it even starts fermenting. We immediately paired our handiwork with warm, freshly steamed pork belly (*Bossam*). Honestly, if you haven’t had fresh, spicy kimchi wrapped around tender pork, you haven’t truly lived. That meal, shared with the people you just worked shoulder-to-shoulder with, is unbeatable. It’s the taste of Korean hospitality and hard work all in one bite.

Local Tips (The Real Tea on Kimchi Making)

If you want to move past just eating kimchi and truly experience Kimjang culture, here are a few things I learned:

  • Timing is Everything: Kimjang season typically runs from late October to early December. If you’re visiting during this period, look for festivals or community events.
  • Where to Participate: Since you likely don’t have a Korean family to join, check out local traditional culture centers (like the Korea House) or temple stays. Many offer excellent, organized foreigner workshops.
  • Dress Code: Wear clothes you don’t mind staining. Seriously. That *gochugaru* stains everything. You’ll be given an apron and giant gloves, but chili paste finds a way.
  • The Kimjang Bonus Meal: If you are attending a Kimjang event, the traditional reward is *Bossam* (boiled pork) or warm, starchy rice cakes. This is non-negotiable. Don’t leave without demanding your meaty prize.
  • The Price Tag: Workshops usually cost between 40,000 KRW and 70,000 KRW, depending on the venue, but you often get to take a full container of your own handmade kimchi home! Worth every penny.

Kimchi is more than just a side dish; it’s the heartbeat of the Korean kitchen. Getting sticky and spicy while making it myself gave me a brand new level of respect for this national treasure. It’s messy, it’s spicy, and it’s deeply satisfying.
